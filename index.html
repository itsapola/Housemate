The lineage

Every housemate piece starts from a design that already earned its place in the living room.

1948

The wall, freed

Poul Cadovius hung shelving on slotted rails and gave the floor back to the room. The System runs on the same rails and brackets, spaced for a cat's stride.

1946

Wood that bends

The Eameses pressed plywood into curves a body could lean on. The Column's walnut arms carry that line from base to perch.

1957

One stem

Saarinen cleared the clutter of legs with a single pedestal. The Cocoon and The Column each stand on one base with nothing to trip over.

1963

A room inside a room

Aarnio's sphere wrapped the sitter in quiet. The Cocoon scales that shell to a cat and keeps the opening wide enough to watch the house.

Materials

Solid walnut

American black walnut, hand-oiled. The structure of every piece, and the part meant to last a lifetime.

Performance textiles

Fine, matte, tightly woven fabrics and smooth microsuede with no loops for claws to catch. Every cover zips off.

Replaceable sisal

Scratching is meant to wear things out. Posts and scratch pads are designed as replaceable parts, so a worn surface never retires the piece.

Solid brass

Brass brackets carry The System's modules and age to a soft patina. Freestanding pieces sit on weighted bases.

About

Design for shared spaces.

The best rooms make room for everyone who lives in them.

housemate. started with a simple frustration: the furniture our cats loved was the furniture we hid. Carpeted towers, plastic beds, scratchers pushed behind the sofa.

So we went back to the designers who shaped the modern living room and asked what they would have built for the animal on the windowsill. The answer is a small collection of pieces in walnut, brass, and performance textiles, made to sit beside an Eames lounge without apology.

01

Built for the animal

Perch heights, opening diameters, and post lengths come from how cats climb, stretch, and sleep. Comfort for them comes first.

02

Belongs in the room

Every piece holds its own as furniture. It should look right in a room with no cat in it.

03

Made to last

Solid wood and brass for the structure. Washable covers, replaceable pads, and replaceable scratching surfaces for everything a cat wears down.

Journal

Notes on living together.

Design · 6 min read

Why we put the cat tree on the wall

Cadovius solved floor space in 1948. Cats solved it long before that.

A cat surveys a room from above. A floor-standing tower gives it one vantage point and takes a square meter of rug. A wall system gives it a route: up the post, across the bridge, into the box, out along the top shelf.

The System uses slotted walnut rails, so platforms move as your cat ages or as you add a second one. Brass brackets carry the load; the floor stays clear.

Materials · 4 min read

Why we chose a tighter weave

A claw needs a loop to catch.

Bouclé looks beautiful and snags on contact. So we moved every upholstered surface to fine, matte, tightly woven performance fabric and smooth microsuede, where a claw slides off.

Upholstery is also the part a household wears hardest, so we made it the easiest to refresh. Cushion covers go in the washing machine. Platform pads lift off and replace in seconds.

Living · 5 min read

Where to put a cat tree

Next to the window, near the people.

Cats choose spots that let them watch the household and the outdoors at once. Place The Column within a few feet of a window and in sight of the sofa, and it gets used.

Keep the scratching post near where your cat sleeps. Most cats stretch and scratch right after they wake.
